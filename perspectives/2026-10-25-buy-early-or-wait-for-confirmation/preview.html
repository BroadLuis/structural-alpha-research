Buy the Bottom or Wait for Confirmation? The Real Cost of Both Strategies

How much downside can early entries suffer—and how much of the rebound has already happened when market breadth confirms?

Buying into a deep correction can mean entering at attractive prices, but it may force an investor to endure substantial further losses. Waiting for the market to confirm a recovery can reduce some uncertainty, yet potentially sacrifice much of the initial rebound. We examined almost three years of weekly JUNIOR and GDX–GDXJ mining-stock data to quantify this dilemma.

1. The dilemma every investor faces during a correction

One of the hardest investing decisions is when to buy after a significant decline. Some investors anticipate a turn: they identify potential value, examine support zones and build positions before the broader market shows convincing signs of recovery. Others wait for stabilization, renewed participation or confirmation from technical indicators.

Both approaches have clear attractions and disadvantages. The early buyer may secure a favorable entry price but risks entering before the correction is over. The investor waiting for confirmation may avoid part of the initial damage but could arrive after a substantial proportion of the recovery has taken place.

This problem is especially acute in mining equities, where selloffs can be violent and rebounds remarkably fast. What does the investor really risk by buying early, and how much upside might be missed by waiting?

2. Methodology: defining an early entry and a confirmation

The study uses 152 weekly observations from December 2023 to October 2026, together with individual price histories in the JUNIOR and GDX–GDXJ universes. Rather than pretending that historical price lows could have been identified in advance, we use market breadth—the percentage of companies trading above their 200-session moving averages (MA200)—to define two observable decisions.

Early entry: the first week in which market breadth falls below 35%. Confirmation entry: the first subsequent week in which breadth returns to 35% or higher. Both thresholds can be observed at the time; the eventual lowest-breadth week within an episode cannot be known in advance.

For each episode, we measured (1) the worst additional weekly-closing loss for each company between early entry and confirmation; (2) the rebound from the retrospectively identified minimum-breadth week to confirmation; and (3) forward median company returns four, eight and twelve weeks after confirmation.

We follow matched securities where observations are available and report the cross-sectional median. These measures are not returns on an investable weighted index. Some episodes overlap or reflect the same broader sector correction, and their number is small.

A market can be deeply oversold and continue declining for weeks or months. Early buying requires both financial capacity and emotional tolerance for this possibility.

4. Second risk: much of the rebound may precede confirmation

The opposite problem emerges when investors wait for breadth to recover. Mining stocks can rally sharply before broad participation looks convincingly healthier. We measured the median company return between the retrospective breadth minimum of each episode and the first return to 35% breadth.

In JUNIOR, median rebounds reached 12.65% before confirmation in the February 2024 episode and 14.29% in July 2026. The most striking example is GDX–GDXJ in July 2026: by the time breadth regained 35%, the median company was 25.22% above its price at the retrospective breadth minimum.

This represents a potentially meaningful cost of waiting, but it would be misleading to describe that gain as systematically available to early buyers. The eventual breadth minimum is only identifiable after the fact.

5. Three episodes that capture the trade-off

JUNIOR: December 2023 to March 2024

The early signal was followed by a −22.86% median worst additional decline. Later, the median rebound from the breadth low before confirmation was +12.65%. Buying early meant enduring substantial losses; waiting avoided some of that exposure while leaving an initial rebound behind.

JUNIOR: June to August 2026

The median worst additional decline was −8.78%, while the rebound from the retrospective minimum before confirmation reached +14.29%. The initial recovery was meaningful, yet breadth later deteriorated again. Confirmation of participation is not the same as confirmation of a durable trend.

GDX–GDXJ: July to August 2026

The median worst additional decline after early entry was −7.31%. By confirmation, the median rebound from the breadth low had reached +25.22%. This episode illustrates how costly waiting can appear when the market rallies rapidly from depressed conditions.

The relationship is not constant: a larger interim drawdown does not reliably imply a larger subsequent rebound, and a smaller drawdown does not necessarily mean that waiting is expensive.

In both universes, the available median forward outcomes were positive after confirmation. Some recoveries continued for months. Yet an individual confirmation did not guarantee persistence: after the August 7, 2026 GDX–GDXJ confirmation, the median company return was about +10.21% at four weeks but −1.42% at eight weeks.

The point is not that confirmation is useless; rather, it can leave substantial upside while still exposing investors to later setbacks.

7. Is there a single percentage of the rebound lost by waiting?

There is no universal answer. The proportion allegedly missed depends on when we define the rally as over: four, eight or twelve weeks after confirmation, or some later high? Different choices yield different numbers. Moreover, the historical lowest-breadth week is a hindsight benchmark, not a certain real-time entry signal.

We can observe that confirmation sometimes came after median rebounds exceeding 12%, 14% and even 25% from retrospective breadth lows. We cannot turn those observations into a universal, capturable percentage of the recovery that waiting investors necessarily forfeited.

8. Less uncertainty does not mean zero risk

Waiting for confirmation is often described as safer. That claim needs qualification. Reclaiming 35% participation shows that more stocks are back above their long-term moving averages. It does not prove that further selling has ended. Breadth can recover temporarily and later weaken again; conversely, a deeply oversold market can rebound violently before any confirmation appears.

10. Limitations of the research

The historical window is approximately three years, not multiple independent mining cycles. Weeks of weak breadth cluster into larger corrections, leaving far fewer independent episodes than there are weekly observations.

Company medians are not the performance of a real investable portfolio. The worst drawdown is based on weekly closes, so intraday losses could have been larger. Historical universes include ticker changes and corporate transactions whose full final consideration is not always available from closing-price series. The 35% breadth threshold is an operational research definition, not a universal dividing line between bull and bear markets.

No commissions, trading spreads, market impact, dynamic portfolio weights, executable exits or comprehensive capital allocation are modeled. Results are descriptive and exploratory, not a verified automatic entry strategy.

11. Conclusions: the real cost of buying early and waiting

Early buying can secure attractive prices when the market is severely oversold, but it also means accepting the possibility of substantial further declines. In some episodes, the median company lost more than 20% after the first breadth-warning signal before participation visibly recovered.

Waiting for confirmation may avoid some of that early exposure, but the opportunity cost can be considerable. In certain episodes, the median stock had already rebounded more than 20% from the retrospective breadth minimum before the chosen confirmation appeared.

The decision is not simply between a risky strategy and a safe one. The early investor accepts timing uncertainty; the waiting investor accepts the possibility of a higher entry price. Neither risk disappears because of a single breadth rule.

12. Final perspective: the perfect entry is an illusion

There is an unavoidable tension in seeking the perfect market entry. We want to buy near the low, but we also want enough evidence that the low is behind us. Those two conditions rarely arrive at the same time. The most attractive prices often coincide with the greatest uncertainty; as uncertainty eases, prices may already have recovered substantially.

Buying early means accepting that the market may keep falling. Waiting for confirmation means accepting that part of the recovery may happen without you.

Our results do not demonstrate that one approach is universally superior. They quantify the trade-off and show why it is so hard to resolve during deep corrections. The goal is not to eliminate both disadvantages—something no simple indicator can promise—but to understand which uncertainty fits an investor's horizon, financial resilience and knowledge of the underlying companies.

Ultimately, waiting does not always cost returns, just as buying early does not always entail additional losses. Both costs are real, variable and impossible to know fully in advance.
